# Monday MedTech: week of October 26, 2026 (voiceover)

Authoritative spoken source is `script.json`.

- s1: On October twentieth, the F-D-A added five more Olympus endoscope models to the list of devices it won't let into the country. Fifty-eight were already on that list.
- s2: Two more companies got a verdict from outside that same week. Edwards Lifesciences won F-D-A approval to repair a second heart valve with one of its devices, and a private equity firm agreed to buy Qiagen for less than comparable deals usually go for.
- s3: The Olympus import alert started in June twenty twenty-five. It already covered scopes for the urinary tract, the lungs and the abdomen, plus the machines that clean them. This update adds certain enteroscopes, which look inside the small intestine, and a capsule camera the patient swallows. Hospitals that already own these devices can keep using them as long as they're working.
- s4: F-D-A inspectors had already been through six Olympus sites, in Japan, Germany and Minnesota. On October seventh, the agency posted what they found in a warning letter. At the Shirakawa plant in Japan, a risk report left out catastrophic hazards, among them patient death and air embolism, where air gets into the bloodstream. It still left them out after a death had been confirmed. The Minnesota site reworked devices after reports that a needle came loose, then shipped them without adequate proof the rework fixed it. And at Hachioji, in Japan, Olympus didn't identify corrective actions after a death that followed a bronchoscope procedure.
- s5: The F-D-A says its concerns remain despite what it calls extensive and ongoing efforts by Olympus. Olympus says it doesn't expect the import alerts to have a material effect on its customers, or on its results for the fiscal year that ends in March.
- s6: The day after the Olympus update, Edwards Lifesciences announced F-D-A approval of its PASCAL Precision system for repairing a leaking tricuspid valve, the valve on the right side of the heart. The same device was already approved for the mitral valve, on the left side.
- s7: The approval covers patients whose tricuspid valve leaks badly enough to cause symptoms, even with the best medicines. Their surgical risk has to be intermediate or higher. And a heart team has to agree that a catheter-based repair is right for them.
- s8: That heart team now has two Edwards options for the tricuspid valve: repair with PASCAL, or replacement with Evoque, which Edwards already sells. Abbott, its main competitor here, sells TriClip for tricuspid repair. But Abbott has no device the F-D-A has approved for replacing that valve by catheter. Edwards says no other company in the U.S. sells both repair and replacement for both of those valves.
- s9: Qiagen got its verdict from a buyer. On October twenty-first, the diagnostics company agreed to be bought by EQT Private Equity, in a deal worth about twelve point one billion dollars, including debt. Six years ago Thermo Fisher was set to buy Qiagen, and that deal fell apart when shareholders objected to the price. This time, Qiagen's boards recommend the offer.
- s10: EQT is offering forty-six euros and fifty cents a share, all cash. That's about forty percent above Qiagen's closing price on July eighth, the last trading day before reports of private equity interest came out.
- s11: Analysts at William Blair put that price at about fifteen times what they expect Qiagen to earn in twenty twenty-seven, on an E-B-I-T-D-A basis. Comparable life sciences deals usually go for the high teens to low twenties. William Blair blamed the lower multiple on growth: some Qiagen businesses gain, and pressure in others cancels those gains out.
- s12: Jonathan Pratt became Qiagen's chief executive less than two months before the deal. He says private ownership will let the company move faster and put its money behind the businesses with the most room to grow. The deal is expected to close in mid twenty twenty-seven, if shareholders and regulators approve it.
- s13: Three outsiders could look at my company this month: an F-D-A inspector reading the risk file, doctors weighing my device against the alternatives, and a buyer pricing my growth. Which one finds the thing I haven't fixed yet?
